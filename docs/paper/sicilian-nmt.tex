\begin{abstract}
Sicilian is a low-resource Romance language that the massively multilingual systems now
covering it learned from noisy, non-standardised web text, so that what it lacks is no longer
coverage but quality; in order to obtain better translations we redevelop the small neural
machine translation (NMT) system that \citet{wdowiak2022loresnmt} customized for Sicilian. We
rebuild the parallel-corpus pipeline on sentence-embedding extraction and alignment (PyMuPDF and
LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}), we assemble a reproducible, deduplicated Sicilian--English dataset
with a held-out \emph{literary} test set (not FLORES), and we port the paper's tokenization and
desinence-biasing recipe to a Perl-free stack. We evaluate both from-scratch Sockeye baselines
and the pretrained NLLB-200 model \citep{nllb2022} adapted with LoRA \citep{hu2021lora}: on our
harder held-out test, Sicilian$\rightarrow$English rises from a 5.27 BLEU floor to 31.43 with a
bidirectionally fine-tuned NLLB-1.3B, above Wdowiak's published baseline (29.1), while the
reverse direction nearly doubles (9.89$\rightarrow$18.73). We further include Italian as a
closely related, higher-resource bridge language (\texttt{ita\_Latn}) that NLLB supports
natively for it$\leftrightarrow$scn, and which we evaluate and fine-tune on a frozen WikiMatrix
it--scn split; the full pipeline, dataset assembly and evaluation are reproducible.
\end{abstract}

%%  INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}
Languages without an army and navy often lack a standard orthography, so that, absent settled
spelling rules and conventions, their written texts exhibit great variability, at times even
within a single local dialect, which in turn inhibits the ability of conventional metrics to
assess model performance \citep{aepli2023benchmark}. Even a language like Sicilian, for which
published dictionaries have presented a consistent orthography for several hundred years
\citep{pasqualino1785,mortillaro1876,nicotra1883,traina1868}, can exhibit an orthographic
diversity that, once incorporated into a training dataset, prevents a model from learning to
generate consistent text.

During the 13\textsuperscript{th} century the Sicilian School of Poets at the court of Emperor
Frederick~II created the first literary standard in Italy, and their poetry inspired Dante to
write in his native Florentine, which later became today's Italian; following in their
footsteps, poets such as Antonio Veneziano (1543--1593), Giovanni Meli (1740--1815), Domenico
Tempio (1750--1821), Ignazio Buttitta (1899--1997) and Salvatore Camilleri (1921--2021)
continued the literary tradition, writing in a fairly consistent manner. Writing, however, is
not speaking: Giuseppe Pitrè (1841--1916) developed a parallel popular tradition that recorded
the common people in their own words and in the sound of their own words.

One can hear echoes of the popular tradition in the daily speech of millions of people in
Sicily, Calabria and Puglia, as well as in diaspora communities abroad, and echoes of the
literary tradition in books, journals and pages of the internet; the publicly available
Sicilian text, however, exhibits an orthographic diversity that once prevented practitioners
from assembling datasets of parallel text and, for a time, kept Sicilian out of every major
translation service. Today Sicilian is included in massively multilingual models such as
NLLB-200 \citep{nllb2022}, but the coverage is of low quality, since the Sicilian training text
was mined from non-standardised web sources and the model has, in effect, learned a
non-standard orthography (Section~\ref{sec:standardization}); the barrier for Sicilian has thus
shifted from absence to quality.

High-quality material does exist, above all in the bilingual literary journal \emph{Arba Sicula}
(published since 1979), which \citet{wdowiak2022loresnmt} used to build the first Sicilian neural
machine translation (NMT) system; that work showed that a fairly good translation model can be
trained in a low-resource setting, at the cost of a labour-intensive process in which the
sentences were aligned by hand with a recipe tailored to the low-resource, morphologically rich
case. The present work rebuilds that effort with maintained, mostly CPU-only tooling, and seeks
better quality through modern pretrained models.

%%  BACKGROUND

\section{Background}
NMT models translation as a conditional language model, in which an encoder reads the source
$x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ and a decoder factorises the target from left to right,
$p_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t} p_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t},x)$, the model being trained by maximising
the log-likelihood of a parallel corpus and decoded with beam search; the dominant
parameterisation is the Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention}, which replaces recurrence with
scaled dot-product attention, $\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})V$,
run in parallel heads.

With little data the default Transformer overfits, so the low-resource recipe of
\citet{sennrich2019loresnmt} prescribes a smaller, heavily regularised model, with high dropout
\citep{srivastava2014dropout} and small subword vocabularies; subword segmentation by byte-pair
encoding \citep{sennrich2016subword} gives an open vocabulary and shares statistics across word
forms, which is essential for a morphologically rich language. Further gains come from linguistic
input features \citep{sennrich2016linguistic}, from back-translation of monolingual data
\citep{sennrich2015backtrans}, and from multilingual training with a target-language token
\citep{johnson2017zeroshot} that enables bridging through related high-resource languages
\citep{fan2020beyond, arivazhagan2019massively}; low-resource MT remains hard for exactly these
reasons \citep{koehnknowles2017}.

%%  DATA PIPELINE

\section{Data Pipeline}
\subsection{Extraction from PDFs}
We extract clean text from the Arba Sicula issue PDFs with PyMuPDF (no pdflatex round-trip).
Each page is assigned a language from its share of Sicilian stop-words and diacritics; the
journal sets Sicilian and English on facing pages, giving a natural even/odd prior, so we form
facing candidate pairs. Pages are segmented into sentences with \texttt{pysbd} (per language),
filtering ``furniture'' (all-caps headers, page numbers, captions). The procedure recovers
parallel pages the manual selection had skipped.

%%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%

%%  (sub) sentence alignment

\subsection{Sentence alignment}
\label{sec:alignment}
We embed every sentence with LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, a language-agnostic dual encoder, and
build the cosine-similarity matrix $S_{ij}=\langle u_i,v_j\rangle/(\lVert u_i\rVert\lVert
v_j\rVert)$. A facing pair is confirmed only if its mean best-match similarity exceeds
$\tau_{\text{page}}$; sentences are then aligned by a monotonic dynamic program over $S$
allowing $1$--$1$, $1$--$2$ and $2$--$1$ moves with a null penalty, in the spirit of Vecalign
\citep{thompson2019vecalign} but without a bilingual dictionary or hunalign. The thresholds
$(\tau_{\text{page}},\tau_{\text{sent}})=(0.50,0.40)$ were tuned against Wdowiak's hand-aligned
gold (issues AS41--42); the pipeline recovers 14{,}245 sentence pairs from 44 issues, comparable
to his manual alignment, and the \emph{unaligned} English is retained as an in-domain monolingual
pool for back-translation.

%% \section{Standardization, Tokenization and Subwords}
\section{Standardization}
\label{sec:standardization}
%%
Although Sicilian does not have a single official orthography, a common literary standard has
existed for several hundred years and provides a rough consistency, which we can refine because
the majority of the Sicilian sentences in the NLLB dataset were written in that standard.
Roughly one-third of the sentences the NLLB team collected were nonetheless written in local
dialect, some of them in Corsican, and very few of the pairs carried a valid translation; we
suspect that these problems trace back to seed data that is itself inconsistent with the literary
standard.

%%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%  %%

%%  (sub) why Sicilian lives in the Romance neighborhood

\subsection{Why Sicilian lives in the Romance neighbourhood}
\label{sec:dataquality}
A pretrained multilingual encoder needs no explicit Sicilian support to \emph{read} Sicilian.
The representations it learns are not a shared interlingua that maps every language into one
abstract, universal meaning space; instead they cluster languages by linguistic similarity
\citep{kudugunta2019investigating} and organise around surface-form cues \citep{verma2026script}.
For Sicilian and Italian, which are close on both counts, the two accounts agree: Sicilian lands
in the Italian--Romance region either way.

The surface form of literary Sicilian is close to Italian, so reading it works well.
On the multi-parallel FLORES-200 devtest \citep{nllb2022}, LaBSE
\citep{feng2022labse} retrieves the correct English translation of a Sicilian sentence
99\% of the time (precision@1, Sicilian$\rightarrow$English), one point behind \emph{supported}
Italian (100\%); and, discounting English (LaBSE's pivot, to which everything is close), each
Sicilian sentence's nearest neighbour is overwhelmingly Italian (18\%, twice the
next Romance language). In lexicon and spelling Sicilian is close enough to Italian that the
model treats it almost as a variety of Italian.

The seed-data failure, however, is in the \emph{opposite} direction, namely the retrieval of
the Sicilian translation of an English sentence.
%% Generating Sicilian
%% (English$\rightarrow$Sicilian)
%% reproduces whatever surface form the training data carried.
Romance languages share a lot of vocabulary and grammar, so when a model
retrieves a Sicilian, Italian or Corsican translation of an English sentence,
the translation that it selects will reflect the surface-form cues in its training data.
Sometimes the NLLB seed data's surface form reflects the literary language in
Sicilian dictionaries, but other times it reflects local pronunciations, which
(we suppose) is what caused their model to frequently retrieve Corsican-language sentences
when assembling the NLLB's Sicilian corpus.

%% Because Corsican, Sicilian and Italian sit so close together, a
%% Corsican--English model already appears to ``work'' on Sicilian, so a selection process that
%% trusts that signal admits Corsican and other Romance web text as ``Sicilian''---the roughly
%% one-third dialectal, partly Corsican portion noted above---and a model trained on it learns to
%% emit that Corsican-tinged surface form when asked for Sicilian.
Since multilingual models encode
script and surface form over deeper linguistic structure \citep{verma2026script}, the two
directions diverge for the same reason: an Italianate surface form retrieves the right English,
while Corsican-looking seed data makes the model generate the wrong Sicilian. The proximity is
thus double-edged: it is exactly what lets our own LaBSE-based aligner (Section~\ref{sec:alignment})
operate on an officially unsupported language, but it offers no protection against near-language
contamination when a corpus is assembled by transfer alone.

Alignability, in turn, tracks \emph{standardness}: scoring the LaBSE cosine of Wdowiak's
hand-aligned gold pairs per text, standard literary prose (Cipolla's \emph{Don Chisciotti})
aligns at cosine 0.76, with 91\% of its pairs clearing our production threshold, whereas dialect
poetry aligns at only 0.64 (80\% recovered). The non-standard material is thus at once the larger
source of noise in the collected data and the harder to recover, so that curating toward the
literary standard improves quality on both counts; rolling short verses into longer lines
recovers much of the poetry gap (mean cosine $0.65\!\to\!0.76$, recovery $84\%\!\to\!99\%$ when
merging up to three verses), a cheap corrective for the poetic register.

%% \citep{nllb2022}

A translation model needs sentences written in the Sicilian literary language together with
their translations, so we back-translated all of the Sicilian sentences into English and Italian
and then scored each resulting pair on the task of translation into Sicilian; since a sentence
written in standard Sicilian scores better than one written in local dialect, this forward
scoring of the back-translations lets us identify the sentences written in the standard. The
procedure yielded 549{,}486 back-translations that simulate English-to-Sicilian translation and
689{,}840 that simulate Italian-to-Sicilian translation.

%%  TOKENIZATION and SUBWORD SEGMENTATION

\section{Tokenization and Subword Segmentation}
\label{sec:tokenization}
%%
Because the majority of the Sicilian sentences in the NLLB dataset were written in a common
literary standard, we can refine them for consistency. We first normalise the text toward the
standard established by Cipolla and augmented by Wdowiak for MT clarity, i.e.\ strict L on the
articles (\emph{lu/la/li}), h on \emph{aviri} (\emph{haiu, havi, hannu}), CI for \c ci
(\emph{\c ciuri$\to$ciuri}) and R retained before a clitic (\emph{farlu}); our
\texttt{normalize\_scn.py} applies the documented spelling rules (e.g.\ \emph{cchi\`u$\to$chi\`u},
\emph{io/iu/eu$\to$jo}, \emph{non$\to$nun}) at a ``std'' level, and additionally uncontracts and
ASCII-folds at a ``full'' level. Since NLLB's own tokenizer was trained on un-normalised web
Sicilian, whether this normalisation helps the pretrained model is an empirical question, and we
ablate it (scn$\rightarrow$en, identical English references): raw 30.60, std 30.98, full 30.98
BLEU (chrF 56.31 / 56.63 / 56.50). The light ``std'' standardisation gives a small consistent
gain (and the best chrF), whereas the aggressive ``full'' preprocessing does not improve over it,
which is consistent with a pretrained tokenizer that expects un-normalised input; orthographic
normalisation is thus only a minor lever for NLLB ($\sim$+0.4 BLEU), so we adopt std by default
and conclude that the larger residual relative to the state of the art is data curation, not
spelling.

We re-implement the Napizia tokenizer in pure Python (verified identical to the original Perl):
it ASCII-folds accents and uncontracts Sicilian (e.g.\ \^o$\to$\emph{a lu}, d\^u$\to$\emph{di lu}),
which cuts sparsity. ``Lever B'' then biases the joint scn--en BPE \citep{sennrich2016subword}
toward the textbook desinences catalogued in the resources Wdowiak assembled to standardise
Sicilian (Dieli's \emph{Sicilian Vocabulary}, Cipolla's \emph{Mparamu lu sicilianu}
\citep{cipolla2013}, Bonner's grammar \citep{bonner2001} and the \emph{Chi\`u d\^a Palora}
dictionary), so that morphological endings survive as units; for instance Wdowiak's
\emph{Carinisi are dogs!} segments to \texttt{car@@ in@@ isi are dogs !}. This cut the vocabulary
from 14{,}000 to 2{,}000 units and produced his largest BLEU gains, and BPE-dropout
\citep{provilkov2020bpedropout} adds subword regularisation by sampling segmentations during
training.

%%  MODELS

\section{Models}
The from-scratch baseline is a Sockeye-3 \citep{hieber2017sockeye} small Transformer (3 layers,
model size 256, 4 heads, feed-forward 1024, weight tying, dropout 0.4, joint BPE $\approx$4k;
$\sim$6.6M parameters), trained on CPU. On top of it we stack lever B (tokenization +
desinences), more parallel text, and ``lever D'' linguistic input features
\citep{sennrich2016linguistic}: a lemma source factor summed into the source embedding,
$e_j=E_{\text{word}}(w_j)+E_{\text{lemma}}(\ell_j)$, whose small ($\sim$900) vocabulary
generalises across inflected forms.

The pretrained model is NLLB-200 \citep{nllb2022}, a massively multilingual encoder--decoder
that supports Sicilian (\texttt{scn\_Latn}) and is steered by forcing the first decoder token.
We adapt the 1.3B variant with LoRA \citep{hu2021lora}: freezing $W_0$ and learning a low-rank
update $W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ ($r{=}32$, $\alpha{=}64$) on the attention projections, with
the base in fp16 and the adapters in fp32. Training \emph{both} directions at once (scn$\to$en
and en$\to$scn) yields a single adapter that serves both and rescues the weak reverse direction.

%%  EVALUATION

\section{Evaluation}
We score with sacreBLEU \citep{post2018sacrebleu}, i.e.\ BLEU \citep{papineni2002bleu}
(\texttt{tok:13a}) and chrF \citep{popovic2015chrf}, on the frozen literary test set rather than
on the NLLB/FLORES set, since the FLORES Sicilian uses the post-2017 orthography and a different,
non-literary domain and is thus not representative of the Arba Sicula material; the Sockeye rows
are scored in tokenized space (the same space as the upstream system, the raw-text floor being
5.27 BLEU), while the NLLB rows are scored on raw text.

\paragraph{Italian directions (it$\leftrightarrow$scn).}
Italian is a closely related, much higher-resource Romance language, and NLLB supports
it$\leftrightarrow$scn natively; we hold out a frozen 1{,}000-pair it--scn test split from the
WikiMatrix data, evaluate NLLB-1.3B zero-shot, and then fine-tune all four directions jointly
(scn$\leftrightarrow$en and it$\leftrightarrow$scn) into a single multilingual adapter, a bridge
in the sense of \citet{johnson2017zeroshot} and \citet{fan2020beyond} (Table~\ref{tab:italian}).
Italian is by far the easiest pair, as one would expect for a close, high-resource neighbour on
in-domain WikiMatrix text: scn$\rightarrow$it reaches 43.5 BLEU and it$\rightarrow$scn climbs
from 17.6 to 27.0. The multilingual adapter costs a little on scn$\leftrightarrow$en
($30.8/17.5$ against the dedicated $31.4/18.7$), so that one can ship either the trilingual
all-rounder or the specialised pair.

%%  (par) relation to the state of the art

\paragraph{Relation to the state of the art.}
We do not beat the state of the art. On his own in-domain, hand-selected test set Wdowiak's
reverse-training system reports En$\rightarrow$Sc/Sc$\rightarrow$En BLEU of 45.1/48.6 and
It$\rightarrow$Sc/Sc$\rightarrow$It of 61.4/62.9, well ahead of our 18.7/31.6 and 27.0/43.5 on
every direction (his earlier marks were 25.1/29.1 for the baseline and 35.0/36.8 augmented).
These numbers are not a head-to-head, since the test sets differ, but the gap is too large to be
explained by that alone: on Sc$\rightarrow$en we only clear his baseline (31.6 against 29.1), on
a harder test, and we trail elsewhere. His reverse-training, we remark, builds a custom
pretrained model from tens of millions of pairs (forward-translation, then back-translation, in
three stages) before fine-tuning on \emph{Arba Sicula}, a pipeline infeasible to reproduce on a
single GPU; NLLB-200, however, already provides that massive multilingual pretraining, so that
our fine-tune is the analogue of his stage 3. We applied the feasible parts of his recipe on top
of NLLB and each adds little, i.e.\ the multilingual Italian bridge, orthographic normalisation
($+0.4$ BLEU) and back-translation ($+0.17$, capped by our 7.6k in-domain monolingual pool
against his $\sim$550k); the method gap is therefore largely closed, and the residual is data,
namely his hand-curated, standardised corpus, far more back-translation, and an in-domain test
set, which argues for combining his corpus with our pipeline rather than competing with it.

%%  CONCLUSION and FUTURE WORK

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We have presented a reproducible, Perl-free path from Arba Sicula PDFs to a Sicilian--English
NMT system, comprising an automated extraction-and-alignment pipeline, the paper's tokenization
and desinence recipe with an added linguistic-feature lever, and NLLB-200 adapted with LoRA. On
our harder literary test set the best model reaches 31.6 BLEU (scn$\rightarrow$en), above the
published baseline but well below Wdowiak's reverse-training state of the art; having applied the
feasible parts of his recipe on top of NLLB (multilingual Italian bridging, orthographic
normalisation and back-translation), each for only a small gain, we conclude that the residual is
data, namely his hand-curated corpus, far more back-translation, and an in-domain test set, which
makes a concrete case for combining his corpus with our pipeline. We also release the serving
code (a local web app and a Telegram bot, a FastAPI service over the NLLB adapter); all code, the
data pipeline and the evaluation harness are released for reproducibility.
